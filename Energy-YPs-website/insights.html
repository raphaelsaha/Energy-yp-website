The 58-Hour Problem: How should we keep the Dutch electricity grid running?

Why a temporary emergency installation in Utrecht highlights the need for smarter regulation and electricity markets.

9 November 2026 | Energy YPs Insights | 4-minute read

I. Just 58 hours of critical grid congestion

58 hours. That's less than 2% of the winter. Yet it could be enough to justify an entire emergency power installation.

According to a recent TW.nl report, Dutch grid operators TenneT and Stedin are preparing temporary emergency capacity near Utrecht to prevent potential grid overloads during winter 2026–2027.

The numbers are striking:

  • 30 MW of emergency capacity originally planned.
  • Up to 58 hours of anticipated emergency support over the winter.
  • 48 battery units and 14 generators planned, providing approximately 27 MW of capacity.
  • The equivalent peak power of roughly 20,000 households.

At the Houten-Oost electricity station, batteries and generators running on renewable diesel (HVO) will provide up to 30 MW of additional capacity. Batteries will be deployed first, with generators providing supplementary support when necessary.

According to Stedin, the emergency installation is expected to be needed for no more than 58 hours in total during winter 2026–2027. These are the critical periods when additional support may be required to prevent grid overloads (not 58 hours of continuous operation of the batteries). The installation could therefore remain on standby for more than 98% of the winter, ready to respond to a handful of critical peaks.

This illustrates a fundamental challenge of electricity grid congestion: infrastructure must accommodate exceptional peaks, even when spare capacity exists during much of the year.

The Dutch government confirms that temporary installations are necessary to protect grid reliability while structural reinforcement continues.

But could better coordination of electricity demand, storage and distributed energy resources, and more flexibility, prevent congestion during these few critical hours, reducing the need for emergency installations?

II. Regulatory solutions: Reward flexibility, not just capacity

Traditionally, electricity network access has relied heavily on fixed transport capacity. Yet electricity demand increasingly fluctuates with weather, electrification and consumer behaviour.

The Netherlands is already introducing regulatory mechanisms to address this mismatch.

Alternative Transport Rights (ATR) allow businesses to accept restrictions on electricity consumption or injection during certain periods in exchange for more favourable network conditions, potentially including lower tariffs or earlier connections.

Congestion management contracts enable grid operators to compensate consumers and producers for temporarily adjusting their electricity usage, even though an easy-to-use mechanism is not available yet, especially for small market participants.

In June 2026, Dutch regulator ACM announced binding agreements requiring grid operators to accelerate flexible contracts, congestion management and improvements in grid visibility.

The next challenge is scaling these mechanisms beyond large industrial consumers.

Greater participation by EV charging operators, heat pumps, commercial buildings and residential batteries could unlock flexibility currently distributed across thousands of smaller assets.

Regulation should make this participation straightforward, financially attractive and technically interoperable. These mechanisms change the economics of grid congestion. Instead of treating every megawatt of demand as permanently inflexible, they create incentives to shift consumption or generation away from critical periods.

III. Market and software solutions: Turning flexibility into grid capacity

The traditional approach to grid congestion is straightforward: when demand exceeds available capacity, reinforce the network. But what happens when the problem occurs for only 58 hours?

Regulation creates the incentives, but digital platforms are needed to connect flexible assets with the grid operators that need them.

Several European platforms already demonstrate different approaches:

  • GOPACS – The Dutch congestion management coordination platform, enabling grid operators to procure location-specific flexibility.
  • ETPA – An electricity trading platform through which market participants can offer redispatch flexibility for Dutch congestion management.
  • NODES – A flexibility marketplace supporting local congestion management, flexibility procurement and coordination between system operators.
  • Equigy – A platform connecting distributed energy resources with transmission system operators for balancing services, demonstrating how smaller assets can participate in electricity system services at scale.

These platforms serve different markets and purposes. In particular, national balancing services cannot automatically resolve local congestion: flexibility must be available at the right network location and time.

The real opportunity is not simply connecting more assets. It's making sure the right assets respond to the right grid constraints, at the right time.

Imagine thousands of EV chargers postponing charging for two hours, commercial buildings temporarily adjusting heating demand, or batteries discharging precisely where a local network faces overload.

Coordinated through aggregators and digital platforms, such resources could reduce peak demand without materially affecting consumers.

This would not eliminate the need for grid reinforcement or emergency backup. But it could reduce their frequency, cost and environmental impact.

The lesson from Utrecht is clear: the energy transition requires more than additional infrastructure. It requires smarter ways to use the infrastructure we already have.
